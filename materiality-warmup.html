Material or Not?

A quick warm-up on materiality using SAB No. 99, codified at ASC 250-10-S99-1

Start with a rule of thumb

A good starting point is 5% of net income.

Net income $280,000×5%= $14,000 starting point

SAB No. 99, codified at ASC 250-10-S99-1, says this number is only a starting point. An error below the threshold may still be material, so size alone is not enough to decide. The real test is one question:

Would this error change the decision of someone who relies on these numbers?

For Yellow Enterprises, those people include:

  • Investors who own Yellow's stock or are thinking about buying it
  • Stock analysts who publish estimates of Yellow's earnings
  • Yellow's board of directors and audit committee

Yellow is publicly traded and files with the SEC, so SAB No. 99 applies directly.

Choose a benchmark

Six quick steps before the review.

Step 1: Calculate the options

The same company can produce very different starting points depending on the benchmark. Calculate each threshold below. The percentages are common examples taken from ranges accountants use. Firms set their own, and SAB No. 99 doesn't set any.

BenchmarkCalculationYour threshold
Net income$280,000 × 5%
Income before income taxes$365,000 × 5%
Sales$2,450,000 × 0.5%
Total assets$1,855,000 × 1%

Step 2: Pick one for Yellow

Your four thresholds run from $12,250 to $18,550. Which one fits Yellow best? Think about who reads Yellow's statements and what they watch most closely.

Check your thinking

Yellow is a profitable public company, and its investors and stock analysts focus on earnings. That makes an earnings benchmark a common choice. Net income and income before income taxes are both easy to defend.

The review uses 5% of net income, or $14,000. A different choice may also be appropriate if you explain why the benchmark fits the people reading the statements.

What a strong different choice looks like. Say you pick 5% of income before income taxes, or $18,250. You could explain your choice this way: Yellow’s investors watch earnings, but income tax expense may rise or fall for reasons unrelated to how Yellow ran its business, such as a change in tax law. Income before taxes shows earnings without that swing. This explanation ties the choice to what Yellow’s readers rely on.

What a weak choice looks like. “I picked total assets because that gives the biggest threshold.” That picks a number to make errors look small and says nothing about who reads the statements.

Then use the same threshold for every error. A $16,000 error exceeds $14,000 but falls below $18,250. The benchmark changes the numerical comparison, but your final materiality judgment must also consider qualitative factors.

Step 3: See a choice change the answer

On December 30, Yellow bought $16,000 of equipment on account, and the purchase was recorded twice. Equipment and accounts payable are both overstated by $16,000. Net income doesn't change.

Is the error material using the $14,000 net income threshold? Using the $18,550 total assets threshold?

Check your thinking

Using net income, $16,000 is over $14,000, so it starts out as material. Using total assets, $16,000 is under $18,550, so it starts out as not material. Same error, different answer.

Which is right? This error affects only the balance sheet. The error overstates what Yellow owns and owes without affecting net income. That makes a balance sheet benchmark, such as total assets, relevant here. If you use net income as your benchmark, explain why, such as Yellow’s readers relying most on earnings. Either way, apply your approach consistently and check the qualitative factors before deciding.

Step 4: Try a different company

Birch Lane Hardware is another public company. This year it barely broke even.

Birch Lane HardwareAmount
Net income$4,000
Sales$1,200,000
Total assets$900,000

Would 5% of net income work as Birch Lane's benchmark? If not, what would you use?

Check your thinking

5% of $4,000 is $200. Almost every error would exceed that threshold, so current-year earnings may provide a less useful benchmark. When earnings are close to zero, fluctuate widely, or show a loss, accountants may choose a steadier figure.

Using 0.5% of sales gives $6,000, and 1% of total assets gives $9,000. Either is easier to defend for Birch Lane if you explain why the benchmark fits what its financial statement readers rely on. Another option is to use a normal year’s earnings, based on Birch Lane’s results from prior years.

Step 5: What pushes the choice

  • What the business focuses on. Companies run to earn a profit for their owners are usually measured on earnings. Businesses built around their assets, such as property companies, are often measured on assets.
  • Whether profit is the goal. Nonprofits and government bodies do not operate to earn a profit for owners, so expenses or assets may be more relevant.
  • Whether the figure is steady. When results swing from year to year, accountants may use a normal figure based on prior years.
  • Risk. The percentages are ranges, not fixed rules. Higher risk may support a lower percentage and a smaller threshold, but the choice should reflect what matters to financial statement readers.

Step 6: How errors combine

Some cards show two or more errors at once. These are marked as netting checks. Here is how SAB No. 99 says to handle them.

  • Look at each error on its own first. If one error is material by itself, another error does not erase that effect.
  • Check line items and subtotals as well as net income. Consider how the errors affect amounts such as gross profit, income from operations, and total current assets.
  • Watch the direction. Two errors offset only when they push the same amount in opposite directions. If they push in the same direction, they add up.
  • Then judge them together. If two small errors affect the same account in opposite directions, the net error is the difference. You still need to consider each error’s nature and the combined effects on the financial statements.
  • Be careful offsetting an estimate against an exact amount. An estimate is less precise, so equal dollar amounts do not necessarily support treating the errors as fully offsetting.
See SAB No. 99's own example

Say sales and expenses are both overstated by $22,000. Net income comes out exactly right. But if the sales error is material, the statements are still misleading because anyone relying on sales or expenses is looking at incorrect amounts. Correct net income does not fix those errors.

    Your calls this round

      Review the key ideas

      A small error can still matter when it

      • Turns a profit into a loss
      • Hides a missed analyst estimate
      • Breaks a loan agreement or other contract
      • Increases management's pay, like a bonus
      • Hides a change in an earnings trend
      • Involves a part of the business investors are watching
      • Breaks a regulation or hides an illegal payment
      • Was done on purpose by management
      • Adds up with other errors, including errors that repeat each year

      An error may not matter when it

      • Moves an amount between accounts without changing any totals a reader uses
      • Comes from an estimate that still falls in a reasonable range

      And remember

      • 5% is only a starting point

      Netting rules

      • Judge each error on its own first. A material error can't be erased by another error.
      • Check line items and subtotals, like gross profit and operating income, not just net income.
      • Errors that push the same direction add up. Only opposite directions offset.
      • Judge each error, then judge them together. Opposite errors in the same account leave only the difference.
      • Take particular care offsetting an estimate against an exact amount.
